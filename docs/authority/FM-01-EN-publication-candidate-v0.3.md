# Founder Moves, Business Stays: What Actually Changes in a Cross-Border Structure?

A founder relocates to another country.

The companies remain incorporated where they were. Shareholders do not change. Contracts remain in place. Bank accounts stay open. On paper, the corporate chart may look exactly the same.

But the facts behind that chart may have changed.

The founder may now approve payments, direct staff, negotiate contracts or exercise banking authority from another country while becoming connected to a new tax jurisdiction.

None of this automatically relocates the company, creates a permanent establishment or triggers CFC taxation. It does mean that several parts of the structure may need to be tested again.

**Founder relocation should therefore be treated as a cross-border structural event, not merely as a personal tax-residence question.**

## Relocation changes facts before it changes tax

The first mistake in a relocation review is to begin with a conclusion.

“Am I now tax resident in Germany?”  
“Is my UAE company still a UAE company?”  
“Do CFC rules now apply?”  
“Can I keep receiving dividends in the same way?”

These are legitimate questions. They are downstream questions.

The better starting point is:

> **What actually changed when the founder moved?**

A factual map normally asks where the founder and directors live, where ongoing management is carried out, who controls bank accounts and contracts, where staff perform their functions, what foreign entities the founder owns or controls, how owner payments are made and whether governance records still reflect the operating reality.

Only then should domestic law, treaty rules and the relevant banking or reporting regimes be applied.

## 1. Start with personal residence — but do not stop there

Personal residence is usually the most visible issue. But a simple “183-day rule” is not an adequate cross-border analysis.

Each jurisdiction first applies its own domestic residence rules. If two states both treat the individual as resident, an applicable double-tax treaty may then contain tie-breaker rules. The OECD Model, for example, moves through factors including permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode and nationality before, if necessary, competent-authority agreement.

The distinction matters:

> **Domestic-law residence and treaty residence are related, but they are not necessarily the same legal question.**

A treaty tie-breaker determines residence for purposes of that treaty. It should not be described as automatically extinguishing every domestic-law consequence in the other state.

If the move is still being planned, departure-related rules may also need review. Some jurisdictions impose deemed-disposal or exit-tax rules when specified connecting factors change. Germany, for example, contains such rules in §6 AStG for qualifying shareholdings and specified events, subject to statutory conditions.

So the question is not only where the founder will be resident. It is which personal tax consequences may be activated by the move.

## 2. Did the company move? Not automatically. Did its management facts change? Possibly.

Corporate-residence systems are not uniform. Depending on the jurisdiction, relevant connecting factors may include incorporation, statutory seat, place of management, central management and control, effective management or other domestic-law tests.

That is why “the company is resident where strategic decisions are made” is unsafe as a universal statement.

Germany provides a useful illustration. Under §1 KStG, relevant corporations are subject to unlimited German corporate tax where they have their **Geschäftsleitung** or **Sitz** in Germany. Section 10 AO defines Geschäftsleitung as the **Mittelpunkt der geschäftlichen Oberleitung**.

The BMF’s 18 June 2026 guidance distinguishes ongoing management — laufende Geschäftsführung / Tagesgeschäft — from extraordinary decisions concerning the fundamental direction of the business. For this German management-location analysis, the inquiry is therefore not reduced to where occasional shareholder or extraordinary strategic decisions occur.

Relevant facts may include where recurring management acts forming part of the ordinary conduct of the particular business are actually performed: operational approvals, payment decisions, ordinary contractual administration, staff instructions and other recurring management acts.

> **A founder’s move does not automatically move the company, but it may move parts of the factual management process on which corporate-residence analysis depends.**

## 3. Dual residence can become more than a theoretical problem

Two jurisdictions may both regard the same company as resident under their domestic rules.

Where Article 4 of the Multilateral Instrument validly modifies a Covered Tax Agreement, treaty residence of a dual-resident person other than an individual may become a competent-authority question. The authorities are to endeavour to determine treaty residence by mutual agreement, considering factors including place of effective management, place of incorporation or constitution and other relevant circumstances.

Under the baseline Article 4(1) wording, failure to reach agreement can leave treaty relief or exemption unavailable except to the extent and in the manner the authorities agree.

This is not a rule to apply from the MLI in isolation. The bilateral treaty, Covered Tax Agreement status, both states’ reservations and notifications and the relevant dates of effect must first be checked.

> **Dual residence is not necessarily resolved by selecting the more persuasive management narrative. In some MLI-modified treaties, it becomes a competent-authority question.**

## 4. Did business activity move with the founder?

Corporate residence and permanent establishment are different questions. A company can remain resident in one jurisdiction while creating a taxable presence in another.

Founder relocation becomes relevant where business functions begin to be performed regularly from the new country — for example management activity, negotiations, client work, staff supervision, contract involvement or recurring work from a home office.

### Home office does not automatically mean PE

The OECD’s 2025 update to the Model Tax Convention added detailed Commentary on cross-border remote work. Under that framework, use of a home for less than 50% of total working time for the enterprise would generally not, on that fact alone, make the home a place of business of the enterprise.

At 50% or more there is **no automatic presumption of PE**. The analysis continues on the facts and circumstances, with commercial reason a prominent consideration.

This is not a statutory safe harbour or a universally binding rule. Actual treaty wording, domestic practice and relevant country positions still need to be checked.

Germany’s June 2026 BMF guidance reflects this treaty home-office framework but separately addresses management functions performed from home.

> **A founder working from home and a founder managing the company from home are not necessarily the same PE fact pattern.**

The BMF guidance recognises that management functions carried out from a private home may create a **Geschäftsleitungsbetriebsstätte** where relevant ongoing management acts are actually performed there.

A founder home-office review may therefore require separate analysis of ordinary remote work, management functions, domestic PE and management-location rules, treaty PE rules and the actual treaty wording.

## 5. Agency PE can arise without a classic office

A fixed place is not the only possible route to a PE.

Some treaties contain dependent-agent provisions. Post-BEPS wording, where it actually applies, may extend beyond formally concluding contracts to a person who habitually plays the principal role leading to contracts routinely concluded without material modification by the enterprise.

That wording is not universal. The actual treaty and any valid MLI modification must be checked.

This can matter where a founder lacks formal signing authority but in practice agrees commercial terms or brings negotiations to practical completion before a foreign director provides formal approval.

Participation in negotiations alone does not automatically create a dependent-agent PE. The frequency of the conduct, the role actually played, the authority exercised and the applicable treaty test remain relevant.

## 6. What foreign companies does the founder now own or control?

Relocation can also change the CFC analysis.

A foreign company may have existed for years without being relevant to the founder’s previous CFC position. A move can bring the founder within a different domestic CFC regime.

At EU level, ATAD establishes a minimum CFC framework for taxpayers within its corporate-tax scope. A founder’s personal CFC exposure must instead be tested under the domestic rules applicable to individuals in the relevant jurisdiction.

Germany illustrates the distinction. Under §7 AStG, Hinzurechnungsbesteuerung can apply where an unbeschränkt Steuerpflichtiger controls an **ausländische Gesellschaft** within the statutory definition and the further statutory requirements are met. Section 8 AStG addresses, among other matters, when income is treated as income of a Zwischengesellschaft, including the low-taxation test and statutory exceptions.

> **Relocation can make an already-existing foreign company relevant to a CFC regime that previously did not affect the founder.**

Ownership percentage alone is therefore not enough. Control, entity status, income categories, effective taxation, economic-activity exceptions and other domestic conditions may all matter.

## 7. Owner payments need to be re-tested separately

There is no single “owner payment” analysis. Different payment streams can engage different domestic and treaty rules.

**Dividends** may raise residence-state taxation, source-state withholding, treaty relief where a treaty exists, beneficial-ownership questions where relevant and domestic reporting obligations.

**Employment remuneration** may require analysis of where the employment is exercised, domestic source rules, payroll, treaty treatment and social-security coordination. The OECD Model addresses employment income in Article 15.

**Directors’ fees or board remuneration** should be analysed separately. The legal capacity in which payment is received, company residence, domestic source/payroll rules and any specific directors’ fees treaty provision may matter. The OECD Model addresses directors’ fees separately in Article 16 rather than under the general employment rule in Article 15.

**Interest** can involve domestic source and withholding rules, treaty entitlement, beneficial ownership and related-party conditions.

**Related-party fees** may create separate transfer-pricing, PE, source, deductibility or VAT questions.

The sequence is therefore: identify the legal capacity and payment stream, apply domestic rules, then overlay an applicable treaty if one exists.

Social security is another separate layer. For cross-border work within the EU, Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 contains conflict rules determining which Member State’s legislation applies, including specific rules for people normally working in two or more Member States. EEA and Swiss cases require checking the applicable coordination arrangements separately.

## 8. Before applying a treaty, verify that the treaty exists

Consider a founder living in Germany with a UAE company.

It would be unsafe to begin with an assumed Germany–UAE treaty tie-breaker or dividend article. Germany’s income-tax treaty with the UAE ceased to apply after **31 December 2021**. Separate arrangements concerning tax-information exchange, automatic exchange of financial-account information and country-by-country reporting remain, but they do not recreate an income-tax treaty.

> **Treaty availability is itself a fact to verify.**

A disciplined sequence is:

**Does an income-tax treaty exist and apply?**  
→ **What does it provide?**  
→ **Has it been modified by a protocol or the MLI?**  
→ **Does the relevant taxpayer, entity or transaction meet the applicable conditions?**

## 9. Does the bank’s profile still match the facts?

Relocation can also create a mismatch between current circumstances and information held by financial institutions.

Under the Common Reporting Standard, a relevant change in circumstances can make an existing tax-residency self-certification unreliable or invalid and require updated or confirmed information under the applicable due-diligence procedures.

CRS and AML/KYC are not the same legal regime. They do, however, interact operationally: the CRS reasonableness test can use information collected under AML/KYC procedures.

A bank may hold a residential address, tax-residency self-certifications, ownership/control data, a business profile, expected transaction patterns and Source of Funds or Source of Wealth information. If the founder has materially relocated while that picture still reflects the previous position, further questions may follow.

> **Does the bank’s tax-residency and customer profile still match the facts?**

This is why banking readiness belongs inside relocation architecture rather than being considered only after a bank raises a query.

## 10. Do governance records still describe reality?

Imagine a company that remains incorporated in Cyprus with a local director formally in office. The founder relocates to Munich and then begins approving payments, setting pricing, directing staff and negotiating important customer arrangements from Germany.

That does not, by itself, prove German corporate residence or PE. Those conclusions require the relevant legal analysis.

But it creates an immediate factual question:

> **Does the formal governance picture still match the operational picture?**

Corporate documents are evidence of governance. They do not substitute for governance.

A credible remediation sequence is:

1. establish how management and decisions actually operate;
2. determine which operating and governance model is legally and commercially appropriate;
3. where justified, change real powers, processes and responsibilities;
4. document the model that is actually implemented.

**Board minutes cannot, by themselves, cure a management model that operates differently in practice.**

## 11. Pre-relocation and post-relocation are different projects

A founder planning to move and a founder who moved eighteen months ago may face similar topics, but the work is different.

### Before relocation

There may be more scope to sequence decisions, obtain necessary valuations, review distributions or transactions, assess departure-related issues, review management responsibilities, prepare banking and tax-residency updates and obtain jurisdiction-specific advice before the relevant facts change.

The objective is not to manufacture a preferred tax result. It is to understand likely consequences early enough to make legitimate business decisions on an informed basis.

### After relocation

The first task is factual reconstruction: when the founder moved; when ongoing management, banking or contractual authority changed; what payments occurred after that point; where contracts were negotiated; and what corporate, banking and tax records showed during the same period.

Only then can the legal consequences be assessed properly.

> **Pre-relocation work is planning. Post-relocation work begins with factual reconstruction.**

## 12. The LEXONYX Founder Mobility Impact Map

A relocation review should not be reduced to one residence test. LEXONYX maps seven connected workstreams:

**1. Personal Residence & Departure Issues**  
Which domestic residence tests may apply? Is there dual residence? Is treaty analysis required? Are departure-related rules potentially relevant?

**2. Ownership & CFC Exposure**  
Which foreign companies or entities does the founder own or control, and which domestic CFC regimes may those facts engage?

**3. Company Management & Residence**  
Where is the company actually managed, and which residence or management tests may those facts engage?

**4. PE & Operational Presence**  
Which business functions are performed in the new jurisdiction, through which people, places and forms of authority?

**5. Owner Payments & Cross-Border Flows**  
How should dividends, employment remuneration, directors’ fees, interest and related-party flows be analysed under domestic law and, where applicable, a treaty?

**6. Banking, Tax Reporting & Financial Profile**  
Do tax-residency declarations, customer records, ownership information and expected flows still correspond with current facts?

**7. Governance & Evidence**  
Do responsibilities, management processes, authority and corporate records describe how the business actually operates?

The map does not produce seven automatic conclusions. Its purpose is to identify **which parts of the architecture changed and which jurisdictions need to answer which questions**.

## The right question after a founder moves

The most useful question is usually not:

> **Which tax applies now?**

It is:

> **Which parts of the structure changed when the founder moved?**

A founder’s relocation does not automatically relocate the company. But it can change facts on which legal, tax, treaty, banking and governance analyses depend.

The correct process starts with the factual change and then applies the relevant domestic and treaty rules to each issue separately.

**Founder relocation should therefore be treated as a cross-border structural event, not merely as a personal tax-residence question.**
