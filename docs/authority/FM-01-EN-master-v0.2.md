# Founder Moves, Business Stays: What Actually Changes in a Cross-Border Structure?

A founder relocates to another country.

The companies remain incorporated where they were. Shareholders do not change. Contracts remain in place. Bank accounts stay open. On paper, the corporate chart may look exactly the same.

But the facts behind that chart may no longer be the same.

The founder may now approve payments from another country, direct staff there, negotiate customer contracts there, exercise banking authority there and receive remuneration or distributions while becoming connected to a new tax jurisdiction.

None of that automatically relocates the company. It does not automatically create a permanent establishment, trigger CFC taxation or invalidate the existing structure.

It does mean that several parts of the structure may need to be tested again.

**Founder relocation should therefore be treated as a cross-border structural event, not merely as a personal tax-residence question.**

## Relocation changes facts before it changes tax

The first analytical mistake in a relocation project is to begin with a tax conclusion.

“Am I now tax resident in Germany?”

“Is my UAE company still a UAE company?”

“Do CFC rules now apply?”

“Can I continue receiving dividends in the same way?”

These are legitimate questions. They are also downstream questions.

The more useful starting point is:

> **What actually changed when the founder moved?**

A relocation review should first map facts such as:

- where the founder and family now live;
- where directors are located;
- where ongoing management is actually carried out;
- who controls corporate bank accounts;
- where contracts are negotiated and administered;
- where employees and contractors perform their functions;
- which foreign companies the founder owns or controls;
- how dividends, salary, directors’ fees, interest and other related-party flows are paid;
- whether governance records still reflect the real operating model.

Only then should the relevant domestic-law, treaty, banking and reporting rules be applied.

The reason is simple: one relocation fact can be relevant to several legal regimes without producing the same result under each of them.

## 1. Start with personal residence — but do not stop there

Personal residence is usually the most visible relocation issue. But a simple “183-day rule” is not an adequate cross-border analysis.

Each jurisdiction first applies its own domestic residence rules. If two states both treat an individual as resident, an applicable double-tax treaty may then contain tie-breaker rules. In the OECD Model, those rules consider factors such as a permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode and nationality before, if necessary, moving to competent-authority agreement.

There is an important distinction:

> **Domestic-law residence and treaty residence are related, but they are not necessarily the same legal question.**

A treaty tie-breaker determines residence for purposes of that treaty. It should not be described as automatically extinguishing every domestic-law consequence in the other state.

That distinction can matter for foreign-company ownership, investment income, reporting obligations, distributions and other parts of an existing structure.

If the move is still being planned, departure-related rules may also need to be checked. Some jurisdictions impose deemed-disposal or exit-tax rules when specified personal or asset-related connecting factors change. Germany, for example, contains such rules in §6 AStG for qualifying shareholdings and specified events, subject to statutory residence-duration and other conditions.

The correct question is therefore not only:

**Where will the founder be resident?**

It is also:

**Which personal tax consequences may be activated when residence or other connecting factors change?**

## 2. Did the company move? Not automatically. Did its management facts change? Possibly.

A foreign company does not become resident in the founder’s new country simply because the founder moved there.

Corporate-residence systems differ. Depending on the jurisdiction, relevant connecting factors may include incorporation, statutory seat, place of management, central management and control, effective management or other domestic-law tests.

That is why statements such as “a company is resident where strategic decisions are made” are unsafe as universal propositions.

Germany provides a useful illustration. Under §1 KStG, relevant corporations are subject to unlimited German corporate tax where they have their **Geschäftsleitung** or **Sitz** in Germany. Section 10 AO defines Geschäftsleitung as the **Mittelpunkt der geschäftlichen Oberleitung**.

For this German management-location analysis, the inquiry is not reduced to where occasional shareholder or extraordinary strategic decisions occur. The BMF’s 18 June 2026 guidance distinguishes ongoing management — laufende Geschäftsführung / Tagesgeschäft — from extraordinary decisions concerning the fundamental direction of the business.

Relevant facts can therefore include where recurring management acts forming part of the ordinary conduct of the particular business are actually performed, for example:

- operational approvals;
- payment decisions;
- ordinary contractual administration;
- staff instructions;
- recurring commercial management;
- other management acts required for the company’s day-to-day operation.

The legal significance of those facts still depends on the applicable jurisdiction.

The broader point is narrower and safer:

> **A founder’s move does not automatically move the company, but it may move parts of the factual management process on which corporate-residence analysis depends.**

## 3. Dual residence can become more than a theoretical problem

It is possible for two jurisdictions to regard the same company as resident under their domestic rules.

A common mistake is to assume that this can always be solved simply by choosing the “stronger” management story. That may not be how the applicable treaty works.

Where Article 4 of the Multilateral Instrument validly modifies a Covered Tax Agreement, treaty residence of a dual-resident person other than an individual may become a competent-authority question. The authorities are to endeavour to determine residence by mutual agreement, having regard to factors including place of effective management, place of incorporation or constitution and other relevant circumstances.

The consequence can be material. Under the baseline MLI Article 4(1) wording, if the competent authorities do not reach agreement, treaty relief or exemption may remain unavailable except to the extent and in the manner they agree.

But the provision must never be applied mechanically. The analysis has to verify:

1. the bilateral treaty;
2. whether it is a Covered Tax Agreement;
3. both states’ MLI positions and reservations;
4. the relevant notifications;
5. the applicable dates of effect.

> **Dual residence is therefore not necessarily resolved by selecting the more persuasive management narrative. In some MLI-modified treaties, it becomes a competent-authority question.**

## 4. Did business activity move with the founder?

Corporate residence and permanent establishment are different questions.

A company can remain resident in one jurisdiction while creating a taxable presence in another. Founder relocation may become relevant where business functions begin to be performed regularly from the new country.

Those functions may include commercial negotiations, management activity, client contact, staff supervision, contract involvement or recurring work from a home office.

But cross-border home working is not automatically a permanent establishment.

### Home office does not automatically mean PE

The OECD’s 2025 update to the Model Tax Convention added detailed Commentary on cross-border remote work. Under that framework, use of a home for less than 50% of the individual’s total working time for the enterprise would generally not, on that fact alone, make the home a place of business of the enterprise. At 50% or more, there is no automatic presumption of PE: the analysis continues on the full facts and circumstances, with commercial reason a prominent consideration.

This is not a statutory safe harbour and should not be treated as universally binding. The actual treaty wording, domestic practice and relevant country positions still need to be checked.

Germany’s BMF guidance of 18 June 2026 reflects this treaty home-office framework and separately considers employees performing management functions.

That produces an important distinction:

> **A founder working from home and a founder managing the company from home are not necessarily the same PE fact pattern.**

The BMF guidance separately recognises that management functions carried out from a private home may create a **Geschäftsleitungsbetriebsstätte** where the relevant ongoing management acts are actually performed there.

A founder’s home-office review may therefore require separate analysis of:

- ordinary remote-working activity;
- management functions;
- domestic PE rules;
- domestic management-location rules;
- treaty PE rules;
- the actual treaty wording.

## 5. A founder can also create an agency issue without creating a classic office

A fixed place of business is not the only possible route to a PE.

Some treaties contain dependent-agent provisions under which a person acting for the enterprise can create a PE if the treaty conditions are met. Post-BEPS wording, where it actually applies, may extend beyond formally concluding contracts to a person who habitually plays the principal role leading to contracts that are routinely concluded without material modification by the enterprise.

The wording is not universal. It must be checked in the actual treaty and, where relevant, against the MLI positions of both states.

This can matter for a founder who lacks formal signing authority but in practice negotiates the commercial terms, brings deals to practical completion and leaves a foreign director only to provide formal approval.

Participation in negotiations alone does not automatically create a dependent-agent PE. The frequency of the conduct, the founder’s actual role, the authority exercised and the wording of the applicable treaty all matter.

## 6. What foreign companies does the founder now own or control?

Relocation can also change the CFC analysis.

A foreign company may have existed for years without being relevant to the founder’s previous CFC position. A move can bring the founder within a different domestic CFC regime.

This is an area where source discipline matters.

At EU level, ATAD establishes a minimum CFC framework for taxpayers within its corporate-tax scope. A founder’s personal CFC exposure should instead be tested under the domestic rules applicable to individuals in the founder’s relevant jurisdiction.

Germany again illustrates the point. Under §7 AStG, Hinzurechnungsbesteuerung can apply where an unbeschränkt Steuerpflichtiger controls an **ausländische Gesellschaft** within the statutory definition and the further requirements are met. Section 8 AStG then addresses, among other matters, the income for which the foreign company is treated as a Zwischengesellschaft, including the low-taxation test and statutory exceptions.

Therefore:

> **Relocation can make an already-existing foreign company relevant to a CFC regime that previously did not affect the founder.**

But ownership percentage alone is not the answer. Control, entity status, income categories, effective taxation, economic-activity exceptions and other domestic conditions may all need to be tested.

## 7. Owner payments need to be re-tested separately

Founders often ask after relocation whether they can continue receiving money from their companies in the same way.

There is no single “owner payment” analysis. Different payment streams may engage different domestic and treaty rules.

### Dividends

Potential issues can include residence-state taxation, source-state withholding tax, treaty relief where an applicable treaty exists, beneficial ownership where relevant and domestic reporting requirements.

### Employment remuneration

Questions may include where the employment is exercised, domestic source rules, payroll obligations, treaty treatment and social-security coordination. Under the OECD Model, employment income is addressed in Article 15, subject to its conditions and exceptions.

### Directors’ fees or board remuneration

These should not be merged with ordinary employment remuneration. Relevant questions can include the legal capacity in which the payment is received, the residence of the company, domestic source and payroll rules and any specific directors’ fees provision in the applicable treaty. The OECD Model deals with directors’ fees separately in Article 16 rather than under the general employment rule in Article 15.

### Interest

The analysis may involve domestic source and withholding rules, treaty entitlement, beneficial ownership and related-party conditions.

### Related-party fees

Depending on the arrangement, separate transfer-pricing, PE, source, deductibility and VAT questions may arise.

The correct approach is therefore not to find one rule for “payments to the owner”, but to identify each legal capacity and payment stream first, apply the relevant domestic rules and then overlay an applicable treaty, if one exists.

Payroll and social security can require separate analysis. For cross-border work within the EU, Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 contains conflict rules determining which Member State’s social-security legislation applies, including specific rules for people normally working in two or more Member States. EEA and Swiss cases require checking the coordination arrangements applicable to the particular situation.

## 8. Before applying a treaty, verify that the treaty exists

This sounds obvious. In practice, it is easy to overlook.

Consider a founder living in Germany with a UAE company.

It would be unsafe to begin with an assumed Germany–UAE treaty tie-breaker or dividend article. Germany’s income-tax treaty with the UAE ceased to apply after **31 December 2021**. Separate arrangements concerning tax-information exchange, automatic financial-account information and country-by-country reporting continue, but they do not recreate an income-tax treaty.

The methodological point is important:

> **Treaty availability is itself a fact to verify.**

The sequence should be:

**Does an income-tax treaty exist and apply?**  
↓  
**What does that treaty actually provide?**  
↓  
**Has it been modified by the MLI or another protocol?**  
↓  
**Does the taxpayer, entity or transaction meet the relevant conditions?**

Country labels are not a substitute for this analysis.

## 9. What does the bank now see?

A move may also create a mismatch between the founder’s current circumstances and the information held by financial institutions.

Under the Common Reporting Standard, a relevant change in circumstances can make an existing tax-residency self-certification unreliable or invalid and trigger the need for updated or confirmed information under the applicable due-diligence procedures.

A relocation may therefore require the tax-residency information held under CRS procedures to be revisited.

Separate KYC/customer-due-diligence processes may also require customer information to remain current. The systems interact operationally, and CRS itself can rely on information obtained through AML/KYC procedures for specified purposes, but **CRS and AML/KYC are not the same legal regime**.

A bank may hold:

- a residential address;
- tax-residency self-certifications;
- ownership and control information;
- a customer/business profile;
- expected transaction patterns;
- Source of Funds or Source of Wealth information.

If the founder has materially relocated while that picture still reflects the previous reality, additional questions may follow.

The practical question is:

> **Does the bank’s tax-residency and customer profile still match the facts?**

This is why banking readiness belongs inside relocation architecture rather than being considered only after a bank starts asking questions.

## 10. Do the governance records still describe reality?

Relocation can also become an evidentiary issue.

Imagine that a company remains incorporated in Cyprus, a local director formally stays in office and the founder relocates to Munich. After the move, the founder begins approving payments, setting pricing, directing staff and negotiating important customer arrangements from Germany.

The immediate conclusion is not automatically that the company has become German resident. That requires the proper domestic and, where relevant, treaty analysis.

The narrower observation comes first:

> **The formal governance picture and the operational picture may no longer be the same.**

Corporate documents are evidence of governance. They do not substitute for governance.

A credible remediation process therefore works in this order:

1. establish how management and decisions actually operate;
2. determine what operating and governance model is legally and commercially appropriate;
3. where justified, change real powers, processes and responsibilities;
4. document the model that is actually implemented.

**Board minutes cannot, by themselves, cure a management model that operates differently in practice.**

## 11. Pre-relocation and post-relocation are different projects

A founder who plans to move in six months and a founder who relocated eighteen months ago may face similar subject areas. The work is not the same.

### Before relocation

There may be more scope to sequence decisions, obtain necessary valuations, review distributions or transactions, assess departure-related issues, review management responsibilities, prepare banking and tax-residency updates and obtain jurisdiction-specific advice before the relevant facts change.

The objective is not to manufacture a preferred tax result. It is to understand likely consequences early enough to make legitimate business decisions on an informed basis.

### After relocation

The first task is factual reconstruction.

When did the founder actually move? When did ongoing management activity change? When did banking, contractual or commercial authority change? Were dividends, salary, directors’ fees or other payments made after that point? Where were contracts negotiated? What did corporate records, banking records and tax filings show during the same period?

Only after that timeline is established can the relevant consequences be analysed properly.

> **Pre-relocation work is planning. Post-relocation work begins with factual reconstruction.**

## 12. The LEXONYX Founder Mobility Impact Map

A relocation review should not be reduced to one residence test.

LEXONYX maps seven connected workstreams:

### 1. Personal Residence & Departure Issues
Which domestic residence tests may apply? Is there dual residence? Is treaty analysis required? Are departure-related rules potentially relevant?

### 2. Ownership & CFC Exposure
Which foreign companies or entities does the founder own or control, and which domestic CFC regimes may those facts engage?

### 3. Company Management & Residence
Where is the company actually managed, and which residence or management tests may those facts engage?

### 4. PE & Operational Presence
Which business functions are performed in the new jurisdiction, through which people, places and forms of authority?

### 5. Owner Payments & Cross-Border Flows
How should dividends, employment remuneration, directors’ fees, interest and related-party flows be analysed under domestic law and, where applicable, a treaty?

### 6. Banking, Tax Reporting & Financial Profile
Do tax-residency declarations, customer records, ownership information and expected flows still correspond with current facts?

### 7. Governance & Evidence
Do responsibilities, management processes, authority and corporate records describe how the business actually operates?

The purpose of the map is not to produce seven automatic conclusions.

It is to identify **which parts of the architecture changed and which jurisdictions need to answer which questions**.

## The right question after a founder moves

The most useful question is usually not:

> **Which tax applies now?**

It is:

> **Which parts of the structure changed when the founder moved?**

A founder’s relocation does not automatically relocate the company. But it can change facts on which legal, tax, treaty, banking and governance analyses depend.

Personal tax residence is only one layer. Company management, CFC exposure, permanent establishment, owner payments, banking records and governance may all need to be re-tested.

The correct process starts by identifying what actually changed and then applying the relevant domestic and treaty rules to each issue separately.

**Founder relocation should therefore be treated as a cross-border structural event, not merely as a personal tax-residence question.**
